How Much BAC Water Should You Add?

Understanding how water volume changes concentration and syringe marks without altering your dose.

The amount of water does NOT change your dose; it only changes how spread out that dose is in the liquid, and therefore how many syringe units it becomes. More water = weaker concentration = more units per dose. Less water = stronger concentration = fewer units per dose. The peptide calculator on this site does this maths automatically; this page explains what it is doing.

Important Safety Rule: The instructions on the vial's own label or leaflet always come first. Section 1

The One Formula Behind It

The straightforward arithmetic connecting powder weight, water volume, and syringe tick marks.

Reconstitution math on a standard U-100 syringe follows one fundamental equation:

mcg per unit = total mcg in the vial ÷ (water in mL × 100)
Worked Line

A 5 mg Vial with 2 mL of BAC Water

5,000 mcg ÷ (2 × 100) = 25 mcg per unit

A 5 mg vial holds 5,000 mcg. With 2 mL of water: 5,000 ÷ (2 × 100) = 25 mcg per unit. So an example dose of 250 mcg = 250 ÷ 25 = 10 units. As detailed in our units guide, each unit mark on a U-100 syringe represents 0.01 mL of liquid volume.

The Punchline Behind the Numbers

In all four rows the dose is the same 250 mcg and the vial still holds 5,000 ÷ 250 = 20 doses in total. Only the unit number changed. Whether you draw 5 units, 10 units, 15 units, or 25 units, you receive the exact same 250 mcg of active peptide.

Section 3

Why the Amount Still Matters: Measuring Accuracy

Choosing a water volume is fundamentally about readability on the syringe barrel.

Syringe lines are read by eye, and being off by 1 unit is easy:

  • If your dose reads only 5 units: A 1-unit mistake is a 20% error.
  • If the same dose reads 25 units (more water): A 1-unit mistake is only a 4% error.

That is the real reason many people prefer amounts that land their dose in a comfortable middle range of units — it is a measuring choice, not a stronger-or-weaker medicine choice. Also, do not add more water than the vial can physically hold, and follow the vial label first.

Section 4

When the Dose Goes Over the Syringe

Understanding how water volume adjustments interact with syringe capacity.

Adding More Water Makes the Problem Worse

If a calculated dose goes over 100 units (a full 1 mL U-100 syringe), adding MORE water makes the problem worse, because the dose volume grows.

Mathematically the volume shrinks only with LESS water (a stronger concentration) or a larger syringe — and any such change must still respect the vial label and the calculator's over-capacity warning, which exists to catch exactly this case.

Section 5

What the Water Amount Does NOT Change

Key facts that remain constant regardless of the water amount added.

  • It does not change the total peptide in the vial (still 5 mg in the example).
  • It does not change the total number of doses.
  • It does not change the storage rules — a mixed vial still lives in the fridge and is discarded 28–30 days after mixing (see our guides on bacteriostatic water and our storage guide).
  • It does not make a dose stronger or weaker — the same mcg is the same mcg.
Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Direct answers to the most common reconstitution water questions.

Is 2 mL of water always the right amount?

No. There is no universal amount; the label comes first. 2 mL is simply a common middle choice because it often lands doses at easy-to-read unit numbers — the table above shows what each amount does.

Does adding more water make my dose weaker?

No. It makes each UNIT weaker, so you draw more units — the mcg in the dose stays identical.

My dose came out to only 2 units. Is that a problem?

The maths is correct, but 2 units is very hard to measure accurately by eye — a 1-unit slip is a 50% error. More water would spread the same dose over more units. Recheck with the calculator before changing anything.

Can I add water twice if I added too little?

This guide covers the maths only, not mixing procedures. Follow your vial's label and supplier instructions for any handling question; when the mixture is wrong, the safe maths answer is to start fresh rather than guess.
