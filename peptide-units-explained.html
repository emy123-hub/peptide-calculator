Peptide Units Explained: mg vs mcg vs mL vs Syringe Units

Understanding what each unit measures and how they connect in reconstitution math.

Almost every beginner mistake with peptides is really a units mistake. When preparing a peptide solution, four different measurements are involved at once: the vial speaks in milligrams (mg), the dose is written in micrograms (mcg), the bacteriostatic water is measured in milliliters (mL), and the syringe barrel displays "units".

This guide explains what each one measures and how they connect, so the calculator's answers stop looking like magic. When you understand the distinction between dry powder weight, liquid volume, and syringe markings, calculating your syringe draw becomes simple and clear.

Important Safety Rule: This guide explains only unit meanings and conversion maths — what mg, mcg, mL, and syringe units measure, and how to convert between them. It does not give any dose recommendation, describe injection technique, or make any health or benefit claims. All numbers in this article are illustrative examples for maths only.

The 4 Units at a Glance

Each unit measures a completely different physical property.

Scroll sideways on mobile to view full table
Unit Measurement Type What It Measures
mg (milligram) Weight (Mass) The weight of the powder in the vial. Vial sizes are written in mg: 5 mg, 10 mg.
mcg (microgram) Weight (Mass) The weight of a single dose. Doses are usually written in mcg because they are much smaller than a whole vial.
mL (millilitre) Liquid Volume A volume of liquid: how much water you add, and how much liquid you draw.
Syringe unit Volume Tick Mark A volume mark on the syringe, NOT a weight. On a U-100 syringe, 100 units = 1 mL, so 1 unit = 0.01 mL.
Section 2

mg vs mcg: The 1,000× Rule

The metric conversion between whole vials and single doses.

1 mg = 1,000 mcg  |  1 mcg = 0.001 mg

1 mg = 1,000 mcg. So a 5 mg vial holds 5,000 mcg of powder, and 0.25 mg is the same as 250 mcg. Similarly, 0.5 mg is the same as 500 mcg, and 10 mg is 10,000 mcg.

To convert milligrams to micrograms, multiply by 1,000 (move the decimal point three places to the right: 0.25 mg × 1,000 = 250 mcg). To convert micrograms back to milligrams, divide by 1,000 (move the decimal point three places to the left: 250 mcg ÷ 1,000 = 0.25 mg).

The Classic Mix-Up: Reading "250" and not checking whether it is mg or mcg is a 1,000-times error — the single most dangerous maths mistake in this hobby, and the reason the calculator on this site always shows the conversion. Mistaking 250 mg for 250 mcg represents an accidental 1,000-fold discrepancy. Always double-check your unit symbol.

Section 3

What mL Measures

Measuring liquid volume in peptide reconstitution.

mL measures liquid, never powder. In peptide math, milliliters appear in two places: the water you add to the vial (1–5 mL), and the amount you draw into the syringe.

On a U-100 syringe the two languages match like this:

10 Units
0.1 mL of Liquid
10 units on a U-100 syringe equals exactly 0.1 mL.
50 Units
0.5 mL of Liquid
50 units on a U-100 syringe equals exactly 0.5 mL.
100 Units
1.0 mL of Liquid
100 units on a U-100 syringe equals exactly 1.0 mL (a full syringe).
1 Unit
0.01 mL of Liquid
Every individual small tick mark represents exactly 0.01 mL.
Section 4

What a Syringe Unit Really Is

Understanding syringe units as slices of volume determined by concentration.

A unit is a slice of volume — how much mcg it contains depends entirely on the mix. A syringe barrel has no fixed dose strength of its own until you draw liquid from a dissolved vial.

Show the connection:

concentration in mcg/mL ÷ 100 = mcg per unit (U-100)

Two mixes of the same 5 mg vial make the point:

  • Mixed with 2 mL: 5 mg ÷ 2 mL = 2.5 mg/mL = 2,500 mcg/mL. 2,500 ÷ 100 → 25 mcg per unit.
  • Mixed with 1 mL: 5 mg ÷ 1 mL = 5.0 mg/mL = 5,000 mcg/mL. 5,000 ÷ 100 → 50 mcg per unit.

Because the 1 mL mix has half as much water, each unit is twice as strong as in the 2 mL mix.

Important Warning: Syringe "units" are NOT IU (international units) — IU measures biological activity for certain medicines, while syringe units only measure volume. Never treat them as the same number.
Section 5

One Dose, Start to Finish

Follow one worked calculation from powder in the vial to syringe markings.

Consider a 5 mg vial mixed with 2 mL of BAC water:

Step 1

Calculate Concentration in mg/mL

concentration = 5 mg ÷ 2 mL = 2.5 mg/mL

Each milliliter of liquid contains 2.5 milligrams of dissolved peptide.

Step 2

Convert to Micrograms (mcg/mL)

in mcg that is 2,500 mcg/mL

Multiply 2.5 mg/mL by 1,000 to match single-dose units.

Step 3

Find Micrograms per Syringe Unit

mcg per unit = 2,500 ÷ 100 = 25 mcg per unit

On a U-100 syringe (100 units/mL), each unit mark holds 25 mcg.

Step 4

Calculate Syringe Units to Draw

an example dose of 250 mcg = 250 ÷ 25 = 10 units

Divide your target dose by the mcg per unit to find your draw mark.

Step 5

Check Liquid Volume in Milliliters

10 units on a U-100 syringe = 0.1 mL

Drawing to 10 units pulls 0.1 mL of liquid into the barrel.

Then compare the same 250 mcg example in the 1 mL mix: 50 mcg per unit, so 250 ÷ 50 = only 5 units — same dose, half the units, because the mix is twice as strong. You can check any vial size or water volume on the peptide calculator.

Section 6

Common Units Mix-Ups

Four common mistakes that create severe calculation errors.

1. Treating units as mcg

10 units is only 10 mcg if the mix happens to be 1 mcg per unit, which almost never happens. Units measure liquid volume, not powder weight.

2. Confusing IU with syringe units

IU measures biological activity for medicines like insulin or HGH, while syringe units only measure volume. Never treat them as interchangeable numbers.

3. Reading the vial label wrong

The mg on the vial is the TOTAL powder in the vial, not a single dose. A 5 mg vial holds multiple doses depending on your protocol.

4. Copying someone else's units

Their mix is different, so their units are a different dose. Drawing 10 units from a concentrated mix delivers far more peptide than 10 units from a dilute mix.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Short, direct answers to common beginner units questions.

Is 1 unit the same as 1 mcg?

No. A unit is volume (0.01 mL on a U-100 syringe). How many mcg it holds depends on the concentration — 25 mcg per unit in one mix, 50 in another.

How many mcg are in 10 units?

Multiply 10 by your mix's mcg per unit. At 25 mcg per unit it is 250 mcg; at 50 mcg per unit it is 500 mcg. The calculator shows this number for your exact mix.

Is 0.1 mL the same as 10 units?

On a U-100 syringe, yes — 100 units make 1 mL, so every 10 units is 0.1 mL. On other syringe types the marks differ, which is why the calculator asks for the syringe type.

Why does my vial say mg but my dose is in mcg?

Because the vial holds thousands of mcg. mg is the practical unit for the whole vial (5 mg), mcg is the practical unit for one small dose (250 mcg) — the same relationship as kilograms and grams.
