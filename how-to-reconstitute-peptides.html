How to Reconstitute Peptides: A Step-by-Step Beginner's Guide

Understanding the terms, concentration formulas, and syringe units without confusion.

Reconstitution means turning a freeze-dried (lyophilized) powder into a liquid solution by adding bacteriostatic (BAC) water. Because peptides are fragile molecules that break down quickly once dissolved in liquid, manufacturers package and store them as dry powders in sealed, sterile glass vials.

This guide explains the maths behind reconstitution — how your vial size and water amount decide the solution's concentration and the syringe units you draw. Understanding this arithmetic ensures you can calculate your draw volume confidently.

Important Safety Rule: This guide does not recommend any dose or protocol, nor does it describe injection techniques. A dose always comes from your own protocol or prescriber. Every number used in this guide is an illustrative example for maths only.

Section 1

The 4 Numbers You Need

To calculate your syringe units, you only need four inputs.

1
Vial Size in Milligrams (mg)
The total amount of peptide powder printed on the vial label (such as 5 mg, 10 mg, or 50 mg). This is the weight of dry powder inside the vial.
2
BAC Water Amount in Milliliters (mL)
The volume of bacteriostatic water you add to dissolve the powder (commonly 1 mL, 2 mL, 3 mL, or 5 mL). The amount you add controls the solution strength.
3
The Dose in Micrograms (mcg) or Milligrams (mg)
The exact amount specified in your protocol. This comes strictly from your prescriber — never from this site. (1 mg = 1,000 mcg).
4
Syringe Type
This site's examples use a standard U-100 insulin syringe, where 100 units = 1 mL (so 1 unit = 0.01 mL).
Section 2

The Calculation, Step by Step

Follow these four simple steps to find your exact syringe units.

Step 1

Concentration in Milligrams per Milliliter (mg/mL)

Concentration = vial size (mg) ÷ BAC water (mL)

This gives mg per mL. For example, if you add 2 mL of BAC water to a 5 mg vial: 5 mg ÷ 2 mL = 2.5 mg/mL.

Step 2

Convert to Micrograms per Milliliter (mcg/mL)

mcg per mL = Concentration (mg/mL) × 1,000

Convert to mcg per mL by multiplying by 1,000 (1 mg = 1,000 mcg). Using our example: 2.5 mg/mL × 1,000 = 2,500 mcg/mL.

Step 3

Find Micrograms per Syringe Unit

mcg per unit = mcg per mL ÷ 100

On a U-100 syringe, 1 unit = 0.01 mL, so mcg per unit = mcg per mL ÷ 100. With our 2,500 mcg/mL solution: 2,500 ÷ 100 = 25 mcg per unit.

Step 4

Calculate Units to Draw

Units to draw = dose in mcg ÷ mcg per unit

Divide your dose by the mcg per unit. If your example dose is 250 mcg: 250 mcg ÷ 25 mcg/unit = 10 units (0.10 mL).

Section 4

How the Water Amount Changes Your Answer

Why the amount of water you add alters the units you draw.

Explain with the 5 mg vial: with 1 mL of water the same 250 mcg example equals 5 units, but with 2 mL of water it equals 10 units.

More water = weaker concentration = a larger number of units for the same dose. Less water = stronger concentration = fewer units.

Adding 2 mL to a 5 mg vial spreads the 250 mcg dose across 10 units, giving you more tick marks on the syringe barrel for an easier, more forgiving draw. Adding only 1 mL makes the solution twice as concentrated, meaning that same 250 mcg dose is only 5 units, which can be harder to measure accurately on a 100-unit syringe.

There is no single right amount for everyone — it depends on the vial and the reader's own protocol, which is why the peptide calculator asks for the water amount every time.

Section 5

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Avoid these four frequent calculation and unit errors.

1. Mixing up mg and mcg

The difference is 1,000 times (1 mg = 1,000 mcg). This is the most dangerous maths mistake in peptide calculations. Confusing the two leads to a 1,000-fold error. Always check whether your protocol specifies micrograms or milligrams.

2. Forgetting that changing the water amount changes concentration and units

Units are not fixed measurements of powder; they represent liquid volume. If you change from 1 mL to 2 mL of water in your next vial, your concentration changes, and your units must change as well.

3. Using a different syringe type (U-50 or U-40) with U-100 maths

Standard calculations assume a U-100 syringe (100 units = 1 mL). Using a U-50 syringe (50 units = 1 mL) or a U-40 syringe (40 units = 1 mL) with U-100 math delivers an incorrect dose. Always check the markings on your syringe.

4. Copying someone else's units without checking their vial size and water amount

Units are meaningless without knowing the vial size in mg and the BAC water amount in mL. Ten units from a 5 mg vial with 2 mL of water is 250 mcg, but ten units from a 10 mg vial with 1 mL of water is 1,000 mcg. Never copy someone else's units.

Section 6

After Mixing: Two Habits

Two essential safety habits once your vial is reconstituted.

Habit 1: Write the mixing date and the discard date on the vial

Always write the mixing date, the amount of BAC water added, and the discard date directly on the vial label. Reconstituted solutions generally maintain stability for 28–30 days when refrigerated.

Habit 2: Follow the vial label and a licensed professional's guidance

Always follow the vial label and a licensed professional's guidance for storage and use — this guide covers maths only. Keep reconstituted vials stored at proper refrigerated temperatures (36°F to 46°F / 2°C to 8°C).

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers to key questions about reconstitution maths.

What does reconstitute mean?

It means adding a liquid (usually bacteriostatic water) to a freeze-dried powder so it dissolves into a solution whose strength (concentration) can be calculated.

How much BAC water should I add?

There is no universal amount. It depends on the vial size and the dose in your own protocol. More water makes a weaker concentration and more units per dose; less water makes a stronger concentration and fewer units. Use the calculator on this site to test the maths.

Is 1 mg the same as 1,000 mcg?

Yes. 1 mg = 1,000 mcg. Mixing these two up is a 1,000-times error, so the calculator always shows the conversion.

Why did my units change when I added more water?

Because the concentration changed. The same dose spread through more water means each unit holds less, so you need more units.
